Anatomy

What moves week to week, and what never does.

Fixed — never moves

Navy header and cyan rule · the eyebrow (market · week of) · one figure, one sentence, one reason · the Rest of the picture heading with exactly three rows · the Check the tables source line · one button · the other-markets line · the footer.

Rotates — chosen by the ladder

The figure and its unit · the sentence and its four-week comparison · which three metrics fill the rows · which trackers the source line names · the CTA target (terminal for an active reader, public tracker for a dormant one).

Why the source line earns its space

It is a trust device before it is a link. A reader who doubts "34%" can open the table and see every neighbourhood behind it — the same discipline snapshotPublicData.ts already applies to press angles, where each figure carries the /data URL a human can verify it against. It also lands three public backlinks in an email that gets forwarded, which the terminal CTA never can. Keep it small: the button stays the one call to action.

Never appears

No address, no listing row, no individual sold price, no photo. Every figure is a board aggregate, which is what keeps the send inside the IDX §6.3(b) exemption by construction. No claim of novelty — the prior-art gate in trackers.ts applies here too.

Why B is built that way

Seven sends in ten are the province-wide version. It is judged on one number: how many readers pick a market.

The sequence — news, tension, ask

A province-wide figure is abstract, and abstraction does not convert. So B opens with the news, then says the average hides your city and prices that claim with the real spread (23% to 71% — a 48-point gap), then asks. Nothing sits between the tension and the ask; anything that does is a reason to stop reading before the tap.

The spread line is the load-bearing sentence, and it must end in an instruction, not a riddle. "Pick your city below to see its own numbers" — say what to do. An earlier draft ended on "this email cannot tell you which one until you pick it", which is coy, makes the product sound withholding, and asks the reader to infer the action.

The chips are the call to action

Not a generic Save a market link. The friction is choosing, not clicking, so the email does the choosing: every market it can serve, one tap each.

Drive the chips off BOARD_MARKETS — all 15

The set a reader may pick must be exactly the set the weekly can cover, or the chip makes a promise the next send breaks. That set is BOARD_MARKETS (15), not QUICK_PICK_MARKETS (11) — and they are not nested. London is a quick pick with no board row: tap it and next Thursday you get the province-wide email again. Showing only ten had the opposite fault — Milton, Oshawa, Whitby, Ajax and Pickering are covered, and hiding them makes the offer look smaller than it is.

So: chips = BOARD_MARKETS, generated, never hand-listed. Use QUICK_PICK_MARKETS only for the cameras where one exists. A new board market then lights up its own chip with no copy change.

Nothing between the tension and the ask

A "what this looks like for one city" specimen sat here — a quoted Milton lead under "if you followed Milton, this week's email would have opened with…". It was cut. It re-explained a value proposition the reader had just been shown, pushed the chips below a second scroll, and put a city nobody asked about in the most prominent block on the page. The spread line already proves the point in one sentence.

The picker already exists in-app, and is not working

FirstRunRegionPicker shows on the dashboard for anyone with no saved region, and AcceptTermsForm offers the same list at signup. Both ship today — and 305 users still have no market. The missing piece was never the picker. It is reaching people who stopped opening the dashboard. That is precisely what this email does, and it is why the chips belong in the message rather than behind another link to a page these readers are not visiting.
